Choosing a Research Topic

    1. Start with the End in Mind: - Approach your research topic by thinking
         backward from the final outcome. Consider the kind of contribution you want
         your research to make.

    2. Select a Relevant Topic: - Choose a topic that will be of genuine interest to the
         scientific community and the broader research field, especially considering the
         expectations at the defense stage.

    3. Important Caution: - "Just because it has not been done before is no
         justification for doing it now." -- Peter Attiwill, Editor-in-Chief, Forest Ecology and
         Management.

Key Questions to Ask When Selecting a Research Topic:

    1. Relevance: Is the topic focused on something new, current, interesting, or
         compelling?

    2. Impact: Does it have the potential to provide solutions to significant challenges
         within an organization, sector, or society?
    3. Awareness of Existing Research: Are you familiar with the latest findings in this
         field? (Make sure to review and reference top journals and leading research
         studies in the area.)

    4. Final Check in Topic Selection: - Will the results of your research be interesting
         or useful to others in the scientific community or in a related field?

If the answer to all the guiding questions is YES, then you have identified a strong
research topic worth pursuing. Begin exploring and gathering relevant information and
literature to build a solid foundation for your study.

Common Mistakes in Research Proposal Writing:

These mistakes typically fall into three main areas:

    1. Lack of a Narrative Hook: Failing to capture interest at the beginning by
         providing a strong narrative backed by evidence, such as statistics or real-world
         examples.

    2. Unclear Knowledge Gap: Failing to clearly identify and demonstrate the gaps in
         the existing body of literature.

    3. Undefined Significance: Failing to articulate why addressing these knowledge
         gaps is important and how it contributes to solving the research problem.

These mistakes often arise from three key areas:

    1. Unrealistic Expectations: Failing to understand the level of commitment, effort,
         and time required to successfully complete a final year project. Set realistic
         expectations from the start.

    2. Poor Supervisor Relationship: Not establishing a professional, communicative
         relationship with your supervisor. A strong working relationship can significantly
         ease problem-solving and project progress.
    3. Lack of Long-Term Focus: Losing sight of your ultimate goal. Keep the end in
         mind and consistently work to stay ahead in your planning and execution.

Topic Selection

Let us now have a deep dive into topic selection and creation. What are some of the
strategies for Topic Formulation (Wilkinson, 1991)?

    1. Be Brief and Concise: Avoid unnecessary words that do not add value to your
         title. Eliminate phrases such as "An Approach to...," "A Study of...," or "An
         Investigation of..."

    2. Choose a Clear Title Structure: Use either a single title or a double title.
             a. Example of a single title: Optimizing Neural Networks for Real-Time
                  Speech Recognition
             b. Example of a double title: Fine-Tuning T5 Models: Enhancing Question
                  Answering Performance in Low-Resource Languages.

In all these examples, you do notice how they're short, direct, and clearly tell you the
topic without needing extra phrases like "A Study of..." or "An Investigation into...".

Other things to consider include:

    1. Keep the Title Concise: The title should be between 12 and 18 words. Eliminate
         most articles (e.g., the, a, an) and prepositions where possible. Focus on
         including the core subject or focus of the study--the fewer words, the better,
         without losing clarity.

    2. Ensure Research Feasibility: Choose a topic that can realistically be
         researched (given available resources, time, and access to data) and should be
         researched (it addresses a meaningful gap or problem in the field).

    3. Identify Your Interests: - Focus on areas that genuinely interest or puzzle you
         within your field of study.
    4. Explore Trending Topics: - In Computer Science, consider emerging and
         high-impact areas such as Machine Learning, Edge Computing, Quantum
         Computing, Internet of Things (IoT), Blockchain, and others. Choose a topic that
         will sustain your interest throughout the project.

    5. Identify Relevant Keywords: - Keywords help in refining your focus. For
         instance, in Machine Learning, common keywords could include K-Nearest
         Neighbors (KNN), Classification, Regression, Recognition, among others.

    6. Define the Topic Clearly: - Analyze the selected keywords carefully and narrow
         down the scope to a specific area of focus. Ensure the topic is neither too broad
         nor too narrow.

    7. Formulate the Topic: - Review existing articles, research papers, and journals
         relevant to your area of interest. This background research will help you sharpen
         and clarify your topic. Take notes, paraphrase important points, and summarize
         key ideas from these materials--this will greatly assist in writing your literature
         review.

More examples of single (standalone) topics and double topics you may explore further
are as follows.

Single Titles (Simple, Standalone Titles)

Double Titles (Main Title: Subtitle)

In some scenarios, you may have topics where a case study is applied and when
formulating case study topics, it is good practice to follow a few important guidelines.

    1. First, clearly specify the technology or solution you are applying in your study.
         This helps define the technical focus of your work.

    2. Second, explicitly mention the case you are examining--whether it is a specific
         location, organization, group, or community--so that the context of your
         research is well understood.

    3. Finally, highlight the particular impact or challenge your study aims to address.

Doing so ensures that your research is positioned as relevant and meaningful to both

Lesson 2: Proposal Writing

Acknowledgements

The acknowledgements section of a thesis or research proposal is where you formally
express gratitude to the individuals, institutions, and organizations that significantly
supported your work. Unlike a dedication, acknowledgements are formal and focused
specifically on contributions related to the research.
It is not necessary to mention everyone who assisted you--only those who played an
important role in your research process. For example, it would not be appropriate to
thank a partner simply for providing personal support, such as preparing meals, unless
their contribution is directly related to the project.

Abstract

An abstract is a concise summary of a longer piece of writing. It highlights the key
points, clearly describes the content and scope, and presents an abbreviated overview
of the work. Typically, abstracts are between 100 and 250 words, with most being
around 200 words. They often follow specific patterns, which may be determined by
the guidelines of the examining academic institution.
The abstract is critical because it often forms the reader's first impression of the
work--and in some cases, the only impression. For example, in funding applications,
some reviewers, such as members of a foundation's board of directors, may read only
the abstract before making funding decisions. As a result, the abstract must not only
summarize the proposal effectively but also make a strong and persuasive impact. It is,
therefore, one of the most important elements of a research proposal or thesis.

Significance of the Abstract

    1. Enables readers to determine whether the full article is relevant to their interests
         or research.

    2. Assists readers and researchers in recalling essential findings on a specific topic.
    3. Provides an overview of main points, enhancing comprehension before

         engaging with the complete text.
    4. Facilitates indexing for efficient retrieval and cross-referencing of articles.

Core Components of an Abstract

    1. Background: A brief opening that places the research within its broader
         context.

    2. Objectives: A concise statement outlining the purpose or goals of the study.
    3. Methods: A short description of the procedures or approach taken in the

         project.
    4. Findings: A summary of key results or anticipated outcomes, often refined at

         the final stages--such as reporting model accuracy or observed improvements.
    5. Conclusion: A final statement highlighting the significance or impact of the

         results--what they reveal, solve, or contribute.

Essential Questions an Abstract Should Address

    1. Purpose: What motivated this study, research, or project? (Begin with the end
         goal in mind--such as contributing to graduation or solving a specific problem.)

    2. Approach: What was done, and by what means? (Or, what will be done and
         how?)
    3. Results: What were the key findings? (Or, what outcomes are anticipated?)
    4. Implications: What is the significance of these findings? What do they reveal or

         contribute?

Tips for Writing an Effective Abstract

    1. Revisit your article or proposal with the goal of identifying its core components.
              Focus on extracting key elements: purpose, methods, scope, results,
                  conclusions, and recommendations.
              Use the headings or table of contents to guide the abstract structure.

    2. Draft your abstract from memory after reviewing the full document--avoid
         copying directly.

    3. Rephrase ideas in your own words to ensure clarity and originality.
    4. Revise your draft to:

              Strengthen organization and flow
              Improve transitions
              Remove unnecessary details
              Ensure completeness and accuracy
              Eliminate wordiness
              Correct grammar, spelling, and punctuation
              Maintain consistency in tone and voice with the main document

Improving Question Answering in Maternal Healthcare through Generative AI and
                                     Natural Language Processing
In recent scientific research, Question Answering (QA) systems have gained significant

This study proposes leveraging generative AI models to enhance QA performance in

Lesson 3: Chapter 1: Introduction

The introduction of a research paper or proposal provides background information and
sets the context for the study. It shows how the research relates to existing literature
and identifies gaps to be explored. In proposals, especially those seeking funding, the
introduction must clearly communicate the research goals and significance. A strong
introduction includes the research hypothesis1, which guides the research design and
can be formulated by asking critical questions about the topic.

    1. What is the central research problem?
    2. What is the topic of study related to that problem?
    3. What methods should be used to analyze the research problem?

1 A hypothesis is a tentative explanation based on limited evidence, used as a starting point for
further investigation.
    4. Why is this research important, what is its significance, and how will its
         outcomes affect the funders and the research society as a whole?

Chapter 1 ­ Research Proposal Sections

1.1 Background Information

The background section provides the context for the research, explaining its
importance and setting the stage for the study. It justifies the need for the research
and summarizes its objectives. Typically, this is the opening section of a proposal,
article, or thesis.

How Should the Background Information Be Structured?

    1. Historical Context & Literature Overview
              Briefly outline key developments in the field that led to the current
                  research topic.
              If the study is interdisciplinary, clarify how different disciplines intersect
                  and which aspects will be examined. (For technology-focused research,
                  specify relevant subfields, e.g., AI, cybersecurity, or renewable energy
                  technologies.)

    2. Identification of Gaps
              Summarize existing knowledge on the broad topic.
              Clearly state unresolved issues or missing links in the literature that the
                  study aims to address.

    3. Significance of Addressing Gaps
              Explain why filling these gaps matters (e.g., theoretical, practical, or
                  societal implications).

    4. Rationale & Hypothesis
              Provide the logical foundation for the study.
              State the hypothesis or research questions guiding the investigation.

How to Make the Background Engaging

To maintain reader interest (including funders), structure the background as a
compelling narrative around the research's central theme. Avoid excessive detail or
digressions into broad literature reviews. Instead:

     Build a logical flow: Each point should connect seamlessly, guiding readers to
         recognize the gaps your study addresses.

Class Discussion:

Assuming your research is focusing on Sentiment Analysis on Monolingual,
Multilingual and Code - Switching Twitter Corpora, how would you structure your
background?

     Context: Start with the rise of social media (e.g., Twitter/X) as a key platform for
         public sentiment.

     Focus: Narrow down to challenges in analyzing monolingual, multilingual, and
         code-switched texts.

     Gaps: Highlight limitations in current tools (e.g., bias in monolingual models,
         scarcity of code-switching datasets).
     Climax: Emphasize why addressing these gaps matters (e.g., improving
         cross-cultural communication, AI fairness, credibility in fact checking).

Common Mistakes to Avoid

    1. Length Issues
              Too long: Overwhelms with unnecessary details.
              Too short: Omits critical context.
              Solution: Be concise but comprehensive--cover key developments and
                  gaps without tangents.

    2. Ambiguity
              Avoid vague language; assume readers lack deep familiarity with your
                  topic.

    3. Irrelevant Content
              Stay focused on the core problem (e.g., for sentiment analysis, avoid
                  digressing into general NLP history).

    4. Disorganization
              Present ideas chronologically or thematically to show field progression
                  clearly.
              Example: Trace sentiment analysis evolution  current
                  multilingual/code-switching challenges  your study's role.

Key Takeaway: A well-structured background tells a clear, gap-driven story that
aligns with the research's novelty and significance.

Distinguishing Background Information from Literature Review

Many researchers confuse these two sections, but they serve distinct purposes:

    1. Background Information
              Purpose: Establishes the context and significance of the research.
              Content:

                       Broad introduction to the research area.
                       Key developments leading to the current problem.
                       Clear identification of gaps and rationale for the study.
              Structure:
                       Thematic + chronological organization to show field evolution.
                       Focused discussion (avoid excessive detail).
              Referencing: Heavily cited but concise--only essential sources to
                  frame the problem.
    2. Literature Review
              Purpose: Provides evidence supporting the study's
                  hypothesis/objectives.
              Placement: Follows the background (typically after research questions).
              Content:
                       Comprehensive analysis of past studies (both broad and specific).
                       Critical evaluation of methodologies, findings, and trends.
                       Explicit links to how prior work justifies the current study.
              Depth: More detailed than the background--expands on all referenced
                  studies.

Practical Tips

     Background: Think of it as the "trailer" for your research--hook the reader with
         clarity and urgency.

     Literature Review: This is the "full film"--prove your study's necessity through
         rigorous evidence.
Example for Clarity:

     Background: Sentiment analysis has advanced in monolingual contexts Smith,
         (2020), but multilingual and code-switched data remain understudied Lee,
         (2022), limiting global applicability.

     Lit. Review: Smith (2020) used LSTM models for English tweets (accuracy: 85%),
         while Lee (2022) identified a 20% drop in accuracy for code-switched texts,
         attributing it to...

Note: Always cite rigorously in both sections, but tailor references to their purpose -
background = foundational; lit. review = exhaustive.

1.2 Problem Statement

A problem statement is a concise claim that defines the specific issue your research
aims to solve. It answers the question: What is the problem, and why does it matter?

Purpose of a Problem Statement

    1. Narrow the Focus: Transforms a broad concern into a clear, researchable
         question.

    2. Justify the Study: Explains why the problem warrants investigation.
    3. Guide the Proposal: Serves as the foundation for your research objectives and

         methodology.

A well-crafted problem statement is brief but impactful - typically Half a page or 250
words for early-stage research, though advanced work may require more detail.
Structure of a Persuasive Problem Statement

    1. The Ideal ­ Describe the optimal scenario (how things should be).
         Example: Social media platforms should accurately detect sentiment across
         languages to foster inclusive communication.

    2. The Reality ­ Explain the current shortfall (what's wrong now).
         Example: However, most sentiment analysis tools fail with multilingual and
         code-switched text, misclassifying 30% of non-English tweets (Lee, 2023).

    3. The Consequences ­ Propose your research's role in bridging the gap.
         Example: This study will develop a hybrid model to improve accuracy in
         code-switched data, enabling equitable AI applications.

Note:

     Cite evidence (e.g., "Prior studies show... [Smith 2021]") to ground your problem
         in existing research.

     Avoid vagueness--quantify the problem where possible (e.g., "70% of rural
         clinics lack access to...").

1.3 Research Objectives

Research objectives define the specific goals your study aims to achieve. They provide
clear direction and help measure the success of your work. Objectives should be
action-oriented, measurable, and aligned with your problem statement.
1.3.1 General Objective

The general objective is the broad, overarching goal of your research. It summarizes
the primary purpose of your study in one concise statement.
Examples:

    1. "To develop an end-to-end speech recognition model for Fon and Igbo
         languages."

    2. "To design an improved deep learning model for question-answering tasks in
         maternal healthcare."

    3. "To develop a secure e-voting application using blockchain technology."

1.3.2 Specific Objectives

These are detailed, actionable steps that break down the general objective into
smaller, achievable tasks. They should follow the SMART criteria (Specific, Measurable,
Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound).

Example - in case you have a topic below:

Improving QA in Maternal Healthcare with Generative AI

General Objective:
To develop an improved question-answering (QA) model for maternal healthcare using
generative AI.
Specific Objectives: - This section should be in roman numbers (small).
    1. To analyze the current state of Question Answering in Maternal Healthcare
         Domain (you should also add that the challenges in this section will be reviewed)
             a. Explanation: identifying limitations in accuracy and domain coverage.
                  (Literature review + dataset audit)
             b. This will make your first subsection in the literature review.

    2. To study and analyze how currently existing works have improved Question
         Answering in Maternal Healthcare domain
             a. Explanation: This will benchmark existing generative AI models (e.g.,
                  BERT, GPT) on maternal health QA tasks, measuring F1-score and
                  response relevance. (Comparative analysis)
             b. This will be your related works in the literature review

    3. To review the challenges related to currently existing Question Answering
         Models in Maternal Healthcare.
             a. Explanation: This will be the gaps in existing literature.

    4. To design and develop an improved Question Answering Model fine-tuned to
         Maternal Healthcare Questions.
             a. Explanation: This will be your Conceptual Framework

    5. To test and validate the proposed Question Answering model
             a. Explanation: This will be your system from chapter 3 to the end.

1.4 Research Questions

A research question is the specific focus of your study, derived from your research
problem and shaped by your study design. By refining your research problem into a
clear, actionable idea, you arrive at your research question. Typically, these questions
align with and emerge from your research objectives.
Examples: Research questions can be derived from objectives as follows:

Objective: To analyze the current state of QA in the maternal healthcare domain.
Research Question: What is the current state of QA in maternal healthcare?

Objective: To study how existing works have improved QA in maternal healthcare.
Research Question: How have previous QA models advanced question answering in
maternal healthcare?

         For Example:

1.6 Scope

The scope details how in-depth your study is to explore the research question and the
parameters in which it will operate in relation to the study and timeframe.

Example:

This study will focus on [specific area] while operating within defined boundaries to
ensure feasible completion. The research will cover:
    1. Technical Scope:
              Development of a [specific system/tool] using [technology/method]
              Example: "A maternal health QA system using BERT-based models,
                  focusing on English-language queries about pregnancy complications"

    2. Data Scope:
              Analysis of [data types/sources] from [time period/location]
              Example: "Publicly available maternal health guidelines from WHO
                  (2015-2023) and anonymized patient forums"

    3. Functional Scope:
              Implementation of [key features] but excluding [limitations]
              Example: "Will answer diagnostic questions but not provide treatment
                  recommendations"

    4. Time Scope:
              Conducted over [duration] with milestones:
                       Phase 1: Literature review (Month 1-2)
                       Phase 2: Model development (Month 3-5)

Exclusions:

     This study will not address [related but out-of-scope areas]
     Example: "Non-English languages or pediatric care questions"

1.7 Limitations and Delimitations

Delimitations define the boundaries you intentionally set for your study. These are
choices you make to narrow your research focus, ensuring it remains manageable and
relevant. For example:

     Studying only female patients aged 20­35 in urban hospitals
     Analyzing data from 2015­2026 to exclude outdated trends
     Using English-language sources only
Delimitations clarify what your study will not address--not because of constraints, but
because those aspects fall outside your research goals. They help readers understand
the specific lens through which you're examining the problem.

Limitations, on the other hand, are potential weaknesses in your study that arise from
factors beyond your control. These might affect the validity or generalizability of your
findings. Common examples include:

     A small sample size due to limited participant availability
     Potential bias in self-reported survey responses
     Missing data in historical records that couldn't be recovered
Unlike delimitations, limitations aren't choices--they're challenges you acknowledge
upfront. Addressing them openly strengthens your credibility by showing awareness of
your study's constraints.

Key Differences Summarized

     Delimitations = Intentional exclusions ("We focus on X and exclude Y
         because...").

     Limitations = Unavoidable shortcomings ("We recognize that Z may affect
         results because...").
Why Both Matter

    1. Delimitations keep your study targeted and feasible.
    2. Limitations contextualize your findings and guide future research.
By transparently outlining both, you help readers interpret your work accurately and
trust your conclusions.

Real-World Analogy

Think of delimitations as drawing a map ("We'll explore this neighborhood only") and
limitations as weather conditions ("Rain limited visibility during our survey"). One is
planned; the other is unpredictable.

Lesson 4: Chapter 2: Literature Review

Definition and Purpose
A literature review surveys and critically evaluates scholarly sources (books, articles,
papers and journals etc.) relevant to a research problem. It summarizes, synthesizes,
and contextualizes existing work, demonstrating how your research fits within the
broader field.

Key Objectives:

     Situate each source's contribution to understanding the research problem.
     Analyze relationships, conflicts, and gaps among studies.
     Offer new interpretations of prior research.
     Prevent duplication by identifying existing scholarships.
     Justify the need for further research.

Structure of a Literature Review

    1. Introduction
              Overview of the topic, issue, or theory.
              Objectives of the review.

    2. Thematic Organization
              Group sources by themes (e.g., supporting/opposing/alternative
                  viewpoints).
              Compare and contrast works, noting similarities and differences.

    3. Conclusion
              Highlight the most compelling arguments and contributions.
              Identify unresolved gaps or contradictions.
Critical Evaluation of Sources

Assess each work by examining:
     Provenance: Author's credentials and evidence quality.
     Methodology: Appropriateness of data collection, analysis, and reporting.
     Objectivity: Balance of perspective and inclusion of contrary evidence.
     Persuasiveness: Strength of the author's arguments.
     Value: Contribution to the field.

Writing Tips

     Use Evidence: Support analysis with citations.
     Be Selective: Focus on key points tied to the research problem.
     Summarize & Synthesize: Integrate sources thematically.
     Maintain Your Voice: Prioritize your analytical perspective.
     Paraphrase Carefully: Accurately represent authors' ideas and cite properly.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

     Including irrelevant sources.
     Failing to critically assess research design or biases.
     Overlooking contrary findings or alternative interpretations.
     Relying too heavily on one type of source (e.g., only supportive studies).
Note: Write in the third person.

Chapter 4: System Analysis and Design

4.1 Introduction

Chapter 4 presents the analysis and design of the proposed system. It explains what
the system must do, the quality conditions it must satisfy, the data and interfaces it
depends on, and the diagrams used to communicate the system's structure and
behaviour.

Keep the introduction concise. Briefly outline the sections covered, identify the analysis
and design diagrams included, and restate the development paradigm or combination
of approaches justified in Chapter 3--for example, Object-Oriented Analysis and
Design (OOAD), Structured Systems Analysis and Design (SSAD/SSADM), or a justified
data-centric, experimental or hybrid approach. The selected diagrams must fit the
project rather than being included merely because they appear in a list.

4.2 System Requirements

System requirements describe the capabilities, qualities, interfaces, data and
constraints that the complete system must satisfy. They are not limited to the minimum
hardware or software configuration needed to install an application. Requirements
should be clear, relevant, feasible, testable and traceable to the problem, objectives,
system features and evaluation plan. The requirements-engineering guidance in
ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018 applies across different project sizes, products and
development methodologies.
You may introduce the section using a statement such as: "The system requirements
identified for this project are presented in the following subsections."

 Writing Good Requirements

     Give each requirement a unique identifier, for example FR-01, NFR-01, DR-01 or
         IR-01.

     Write one clear requirement per statement and avoid vague words such as fast,
         secure, user-friendly or efficient unless they are supported by measurable
         criteria.

     Identify the relevant stakeholder, source or rationale where this is useful.
     State how the requirement will be verified through inspection, demonstration,

         testing, measurement or user acceptance.
     Maintain alignment among requirements, objectives, diagrams, implemented

         features and test cases.

Where practical, maintain a compact requirements catalogue containing the
requirement ID, statement, priority, source or rationale, and verification method. Do not
invent thresholds simply to make a requirement appear measurable; justify them using
stakeholder needs, the intended operating environment, authoritative guidance,
benchmarks or experiments.

4.2.1 Functional Requirements

Functional requirements describe what the system must do under specified conditions.
They define services, behaviours, business rules, inputs, outputs and interactions that
allow users or external systems to accomplish particular tasks.
Examples of correctly framed functional requirements include:

     FR-01: The system shall allow an authorised user to create an account and
         authenticate before accessing role-specific functions.

     FR-02: The system shall allow a user to search for and retrieve records using the
         defined search criteria.

     FR-03: The system shall notify the affected user after a specified transaction has
         been completed or rejected.

In your own documentation, list and explain only the functions required by your project.
If you organise the system into modules--such as authentication, file transfer, data
collection, reporting or administration--describe the actor, trigger, expected behaviour,
output and important exceptions. The requirement should state what is required;
detailed implementation evidence belongs in the implementation chapter.

4.2.2 Non-Functional Requirements

Non-functional requirements describe how well the system must operate and the
constraints under which it must work. Select only the quality characteristics relevant to
the project. ISO/IEC 25010:2023 provides a current product-quality model that can
help students identify suitable quality areas.

Possible areas include:

     Performance efficiency, including response time, throughput and resource use.
     Security and privacy, including authentication, authorisation, confidentiality,

         integrity and accountability.
     Reliability and availability, including recovery from failures and continuity of
         service.

     Usability and accessibility for the intended users and operating context.
     Compatibility and interoperability with required platforms or services.
     Maintainability, testability and modifiability.
     Safety, fairness, explainability or human oversight where the project can affect

         people or other critical decisions.

Use measurable, project-specific statements. Suitable patterns include:

     Under [defined workload and environment], at least [X%] of [specified
         transactions] shall complete within [Y seconds], as measured using [identified
         test].

     Only users assigned the required role shall access [specified data or function],
         as verified through access-control tests.

     The system shall recover from [defined failure] within [specified period] without
         losing [identified critical data].

For AI/ML projects, model quality, robustness, fairness and explainability may form part
of the quality and evaluation requirements. For IoT projects, relevant requirements may
include latency, sensor accuracy, connectivity, power consumption and reliability.
These examples must still be justified and measured within the actual project context.

4.2.3 Data Requirements

Data requirements apply to all project types, not only AI/ML or IoT. They describe the
data the system needs, produces, protects and retains.
Discuss the following where applicable:

     Required inputs, outputs, data sources and ownership.
     Data formats, structures, validation rules and permitted values.
     Data quality, completeness, accuracy, provenance and handling of missing or

         duplicate records.
     Storage, retention, backup, archival and deletion.
     Privacy, confidentiality, consent and access restrictions.
     Transformations, preprocessing and audit information needed to reproduce the

         work.

Adapt the discussion to the project. AI/ML projects should address datasets, labels,
sampling, train/validation/test separation, representativeness and bias. IoT projects
should address sensor types, units, sampling frequency, calibration and missing
readings. Information systems should describe records, validation and retention, while
cybersecurity projects may focus on logs, telemetry, indicators and provenance.

4.2.4 Interface and Integration Requirements

Interface and integration requirements are also general. They describe how users,
external services, software components and hardware devices interact with the
system.

Discuss the following where applicable:

     User-interface and accessibility requirements.
     APIs, third-party platforms and external data services.
     Authentication or institutional identity services.
     Hardware devices, sensors, gateways and peripheral equipment.
     Communication protocols, message formats and compatibility constraints.
     Timeouts, retries, validation and error handling when an integration is unavailable

         or returns invalid data.

For example, an IoT project may describe sensor-to-gateway and gateway-to-cloud
communication, while an AI project may describe the interface through which a trained
model receives input and returns predictions. A conventional information system may
instead focus on web, mobile, payment, messaging or institutional-service integrations.

 Requirements Traceability

Every important requirement should link back to an objective or stakeholder need and
forward to a feature, diagram and test. When a requirement changes, review the
affected design and evaluation evidence. Discuss significant changes with the
supervisor.

Discussion of Diagrams and Their Purposes

System analysis and design diagrams help students visualise, examine and
communicate different aspects of a proposed solution. They support:

     Understanding and validating system requirements.
     Defining the system boundary and external interactions.
     Representing data, processes, behaviour and solution structure.
     Identifying omissions, inconsistencies, risks and design alternatives.
     Communicating with supervisors, users, developers and other stakeholders.
     Supporting implementation, testing, evaluation and future maintenance.

Use the diagram-selection table already provided in this guide to identify the diagrams
appropriate to your project and chosen paradigm. The table is a guide, not a checklist
requiring every diagram. Confirm the final selection with the supervisor.

OOAD projects will normally use relevant UML diagrams. SSAD/SSADM projects will
normally use context diagrams and data-flow diagrams. AI/ML, IoT, blockchain,
cybersecurity and other specialised projects may require a justified combination of
UML, structured-analysis, data-pipeline, block, network, architecture or state-oriented
diagrams.

 Important Distinction: Context Diagram and DFD Level 0

A context diagram represents the entire system as one process and shows its
interactions with external entities. A DFD Level 0 decomposes that system into its
major internal processes, data stores and data flows. They are related, but they are not
the same diagram.

For every diagram, use the following presentation pattern:

4.4 System Design Diagrams

System design diagrams describe how the proposed solution will be organised to

4.4.1 Logical Database Schema -- Worked Example

 Writing Perspective and Evidence

5.4 Testing and Evaluation

Testing should demonstrate whether the implemented system satisfies its
requirements and whether the project objectives were achieved. Begin with the test
strategy, then present test cases, results, interpretation, defects and retesting. The
general concepts in ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-1:2022 can help distinguish test planning,
design, execution, reporting and incident management.

5.4.1 Test Strategy and Acceptance Criteria

     Define the purpose, scope, environment, test data, responsibilities and
         completion criteria before presenting results. Select complementary test levels
         and techniques because they answer different questions: unit testing checks
         individual components, integration testing checks interfaces, system testing
         checks the complete solution, and user acceptance or usability testing checks
         fitness for intended users where applicable.

     Use black-box, white-box or grey-box techniques according to the available

         performance, reliability, compatibility, accessibility, security or recovery tests
         when the corresponding requirements exist.

     Map every important test to a requirement, feature or objective. Record the tool

     For web-security testing, the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide provides

         vulnerabilities responsibly. An automated scanner or an SQL-injection
         screenshot is not a complete security evaluation; combine suitable review,
         negative testing and manual verification.

     General software and information systems -- report functional coverage, user

Chapter 6: Conclusions, Recommendations And Future
Works

6.1 Introduction

Chapter 6 closes the chain from the original problem and objectives to the
implemented solution and the evidence reported in Chapter 5. It should provide a
concise answer to what the project established, what should be done on the basis of
the findings, and what credible work remains.

Do not introduce new experiments, features, results or citations in this chapter. Every
conclusion, recommendation and future-work proposal must be traceable to evidence,
a limitation or an unresolved question already discussed. Keep the sections distinct:
conclusions state what the study established; recommendations propose justified
action; future works identify research or development that remains to be investigated.

6.2 Conclusions

Begin by briefly restating the problem, purpose and completed solution. Then answer
the research questions or objectives in the same order used throughout the report. For
each objective, state the strongest relevant result and what it means without
reproducing the whole results chapter. End by identifying the project's contribution and
the conditions or boundaries within which the conclusion holds.
 Match the Conclusion to the Evidence Route

     Software and information systems -- conclude using implemented capabilities,
         requirement coverage, user acceptance or usability, performance, reliability,
         security and data-integrity evidence.

     AI and machine learning -- state the selected model or approach, fair baseline
         comparison, final held-out or external evaluation, important error or subgroup
         findings, uncertainty and the limits of generalisation. For high-stakes uses, state
         the role of human oversight.

     IoT and embedded systems -- conclude using sensing or control accuracy,
         calibration, latency, packet delivery, power and recovery evidence under the
         tested operating conditions.

     Cybersecurity -- conclude within the authorised threat model using detection or
         prevention effectiveness, false alarms, coverage, response time and
         resource-overhead evidence.

     Blockchain or distributed systems -- conclude using protocol or smart-contract
         correctness, transaction success, cost, latency, throughput, finality and the
         tested security and network assumptions.

     Hybrid projects -- combine the relevant evidence routes and report the weakest
         important component as honestly as the strongest; a successful interface does
         not compensate for an unevaluated model, sensor, security control or protocol.

Avoid broad claims such as "the project was successful" unless the defined acceptance
criteria support them. Do not claim novelty, superiority, safety, clinical readiness or
real-world scalability from a single demonstration or an unfair comparison.
6.3 Recommendations

Recommendations should follow directly from the results and limitations and should be
written for a named audience such as users, the adopting organisation, system
maintainers, developers, researchers or policy makers. Each recommendation should
state the action, the responsible audience, the evidence-based reason and any
condition or priority that affects implementation.

 Write Recommendations That Can Be Acted On

     Recommend deployment or adoption only within the environment, population
         and safeguards that were evaluated.

     Recommend infrastructure changes only when measurements show a
         bottleneck in compute, memory, storage, network, power or response time.

     Recommend data collection, calibration, security hardening, user training,
         human review or monitoring when a documented error pattern or limitation
         justifies it.

     Prioritise recommendations and separate essential safeguards from optional
         improvements.

     Do not disguise an unimplemented feature, an unsupported model change or a
         personal preference as a finding-based recommendation.

6.4 Future Works

Future works should extend the research rather than repair missing promised work.
Select a small number of credible directions that arise from the limitations, error
analysis or unanswered questions. For each direction, explain the rationale, the factor
that would change, the data or resources required and how the extension should be
evaluated. Prioritise the most valuable directions instead of listing every possible
technology.

 Examples of Evidence-Based Directions by Project Type

     Software and information systems -- longer deployment studies, broader
         usability or accessibility evaluation, integration with additional systems, stronger
         recovery testing or evaluation in a new organisational context.

     AI and machine learning -- larger or more representative data, external
         validation, fairness or subgroup analysis, calibration, explainability, robustness,
         drift monitoring, alternative approaches under a controlled comparison, or
         deployment evaluation with meaningful human oversight.

     IoT and embedded systems -- additional calibration, wider field conditions,
         power optimisation, communication alternatives, hardware revision, durability
         testing or longer reliability studies.

     Cybersecurity -- broader authorised threat coverage, adversarial or evasion
         testing, additional environments, longer monitoring, reduced false positives or
         independent security validation.

     Blockchain or distributed systems -- larger network trials, alternative consensus
         or scaling designs, contract audits, privacy improvements, governance
         evaluation or sustained cost and performance measurement.

A proposed direction is not automatically valuable because it uses a newer framework,
larger model or more expensive device. Link every future work item to the evidence
and explain how success would be measured. Confirm scope and feasibility with the
supervisor.
